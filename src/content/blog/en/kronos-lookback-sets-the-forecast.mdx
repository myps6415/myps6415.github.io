## Why I tested it at all

I had closed a stock-screening line ten days earlier. Institutional-flow
ranking, foreign-investor flow, a multi-factor model — three attempts, all of
them beaten by just holding the index. The written exit condition said: don't
reopen unless the regime turns.

So when [Kronos](https://github.com/shiyu-coder/Kronos) showed up — AAAI 2026,
MIT licence, pretrained on over 12 billion K-line records from 45 global
exchanges, open weights for mini/small/base — my first instinct was to apply the old conclusion
and move on.

That instinct was wrong, and I'm glad someone pushed back on it. The closed line
tested *cross-sectional ranking from institutional flows*. Kronos is a
*price-sequence forecaster*. Different hypothesis class. An exit condition for
one hypothesis is not evidence about another, and reusing it that way is just
motivated reasoning with a paper trail.

So I ran the test.

<Callout variant="info" title="Criteria fixed before running anything">
  Three gates, written down first: the information coefficient has to be
  significantly positive, directional accuracy has to beat a free baseline, and
  the signal must not be pure trend extrapolation. Writing them down afterwards
  is how you end up with a result that is "promising" no matter what comes out.
</Callout>

## The setup

Kronos-small, zero-shot, Taiwan daily bars. Twenty tickers — my actual holdings
plus the candidate pool — at 24 time points, giving 480 five-day forecasts. Each
forecast is the ensemble mean of 20 sampled paths. Every time point sits in
May–July 2026, after the paper's August 2025 submission, to keep the evaluation
window away from anything the model might have memorised.

One incidental finding, since I had budgeted wrongly for it: speed was never the
problem. On an Intel Mac, CPU only, a single path took about 0.1 seconds. My
pre-test guess had been "tens of seconds per forecast" — off by two orders of
magnitude.

## The surface result, which looks like a win

- Spearman IC **+0.187**, p < 0.001
- Directional accuracy **57.1%**, p = 0.002
- **20 of 20** tickers with positive IC

Twenty for twenty. If I had stopped here I would have written a very different
post, wired the model into my dashboard, and been wrong for months before
noticing.

## Kill #1: it was selling me something I already had for free

The forecasts correlated **-0.589** with the trailing five-day return. Kronos
was betting against recent moves — and over that particular window, Taiwan
equities did mean-revert. So "against the trend" was free money that had nothing
to do with the model.

The way to find out whether a signal adds anything is to put the free effect in
the regression next to it:

<CodeBlock label="future 5d return ~ past 5d return + kronos forecast" lang="text">
{`past 5d return     t = -4.74    significant
kronos forecast    t = -0.98    not significant, coefficient negative`}
</CodeBlock>

Controlled for the trailing return, the model's incremental contribution is
indistinguishable from zero, and what little there is points the wrong way.

Then the embarrassing comparison. The baseline — "bet against last week",
zero parameters, one line of code — scored 56.7% directional accuracy against
the model's 57.1%. A 12-billion-candle pretrained transformer, tied with a
one-liner.

<Lesson label="A signal is only worth what it adds">
  Any forecast that leans on a persistent market effect will look good in a
  window where that effect pays. The question is never "is my metric positive"
  but "is my metric positive once the free version of the same bet is already in
  the model". If you can't name the free baseline, you haven't finished
  specifying the test.
</Lesson>

## Kill #2: 480 was not my sample size

I had 480 forecasts and I had been treating that as n=480. It isn't, for two
independent reasons:

- **The windows overlap.** Time points are two days apart; the forecast horizon
  is five days. Consecutive observations share most of their outcome period.
- **The tickers don't.** Taiwan equities are highly correlated. Twenty names in
  one small market is nowhere near twenty independent draws.

Both inflate significance in the same direction — towards looking real. The
standard fix is to collapse each day into one cross-sectional IC, then t-test
that time series:

<CodeBlock label="daily cross-sectional IC, t-test on the series" lang="text">
{`kronos         t = +1.09    p = 0.287    not significant
free baseline  t = +2.88                 significant`}
</CodeBlock>

The p = 0.002 I had been quoting was manufactured by overlapping samples. The
free baseline survives this test. The model does not.

<Callout variant="warn" title="Two tests, one direction">
  Notice that both failures were invisible from the surface metrics, and both
  errors flattered the model. That asymmetry is the thing to be scared of: the
  mistakes you make while excited all push the same way.
</Callout>

## The finding that outlived the test

This is the part I would still care about if Kronos had passed.

`KronosPredictor.predict()` z-score normalises the **entire lookback window**
before feeding it to the model. The window is therefore not just how much
history the model sees — it is the unit the forecast is denominated in. Same
ticker, same day, same weights, nothing changed but the lookback length:

<CodeBlock label="one ticker, one day, varying lookback — actual move was +2.62%" lang="text">
{`lookback  60   →   -0.35%
lookback 120   →   +4.50%
lookback 250   →  -10.08%
lookback 400   →  -29.70%`}
</CodeBlock>

A daily-bar forecast of -29.70% over five days is not a prediction, it's a unit
error. And the model is not hedging: at lookback 400 the p10–p90 interval does
not contain the realised value at all. It is confident and wrong, which is worse
than being noisy and wide, because wide intervals at least tell you to stop.

I tried turning the temperature down to 0.6, on the theory that this was sampling
noise. It changed nothing, which is the tell: lowering temperature narrows
variance around the model's central belief, and the central belief was the broken
part.

The cause is a scale mismatch between the demo and my data. The published demo
runs on 5-minute bars, where 400 bars is about a day and a half and the standard
deviation of the window is small. On daily bars, 400 bars is about 1.6 years, and
normalising across a period that long produces a standard deviation so large that
the de-normalised output lands anywhere.

Which leaves the uncomfortable conclusion for my use case: **on daily bars there
was no configuration that was simultaneously inside the model's design envelope
(context 512) and numerically sane.** Short windows stay sane but throw away
history; long windows use the history and produce nonsense.

<Lesson label="Normalisation is part of the interface">
  If a model normalises over a window you choose, that window is an input to the
  prediction, not a preprocessing detail. Any repo where the normalisation
  window is a tunable and the output is in real units has this failure mode
  available. The test that catches it costs one afternoon: hold everything
  constant, sweep the window, and see whether the forecast stays in the same
  order of magnitude. If it doesn't, you are reading a unit, not a signal.
</Lesson>

## What I did not falsify

Negative results get over-quoted, so let me fence this one in precisely. What
failed is: **zero-shot Kronos-small, Taiwan daily bars, the single numerically
sane configuration available, sampled over roughly two months.**

Explicitly untested, and in rough order of how likely I think they are to work:

- **Intraday 5-minute bars.** This is the demo's home turf, where the
  normalisation scale is sane by construction. I would not be surprised if it
  works there, and nothing above is evidence that it doesn't.
- **Fine-tuning.** The repo ships a `finetune/` directory, and this is the
  obvious next step for anyone who wants to carry this further.
- **base / large.** I only tested small. (large, at 499M, is closed-weight.)
- **Other regimes.** Two months is one regime sample. Mean reversion happened to
  dominate my window, which is exactly what made kill #1 detectable — in a
  trending window the same test would have looked different and I'd have had to
  control for momentum instead.

## Why I stopped anyway

Both surviving paths — fine-tune, or move to intraday — are real engineering
projects. More to the point, both of them push towards short-horizon trading,
and my actual strategy is index core plus dividends on a retirement horizon. A
model that only works at five-minute resolution isn't a tool for that strategy;
it's a different strategy wearing a tool's clothing.

So the revival condition is written down rather than left to mood: fine-tune or
intraday first, and then it has to clear both gates that killed it here —
incremental t-value after controlling for the counter-trend baseline, *and* a
t-test on the daily cross-sectional IC series. No more p-values from overlapping
windows.

<Lesson label="Write the exit condition before you want it">
  The useful artifact from a failed test isn't the conclusion, it's the gate.
  "We tried it and it didn't work" decays into folklore within a quarter and
  gets re-litigated by whoever is most enthusiastic. "It must clear these two
  specific tests, which are the ones it failed" is reusable, and it survives my
  own future excitement — which, given that I nearly dismissed this model on an
  unrelated prior, is the thing most in need of a guard.
</Lesson>
